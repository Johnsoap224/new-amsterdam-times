Quick note first: Google and Meta aren’t technically tech. Wall Street files them under communication services because they make their money from ads, and that distinction is the whole story this week.

### Biggest winner this week

Communication services won this week when we saw other sectors get hit hard. Communication services, which is home to Alphabet and Meta, was the best sector of the week, up 4.9%, and nearly all of it came Wednesday, its best day since April 2025. That needs context, because these same companies have been getting crushed. Alphabet had its worst day ever the week before, and the sector started this week down 15% for the year. So this wasn’t investors falling back in love with big tech. It was a bet. When the election put data centers in doubt, investors sold the companies that need someone else’s money to build, like chip makers and neoclouds, and moved to the giants that can afford to wait out a slowdown. Investors don’t doubt that AI is a lucrative industry, it’s about picking a winning horse, much like the early 2000s and search engines. That bet proved right on Thursday as the sector slipped just 0.6% while tech fell 8%. But winning the week isn’t the same as being safe. The giants proved they can still raise money easily: Meta borrowed about $25 billion this week and Alphabet sold €6 billion in bonds. AI spending is obliterating the free cash flow of the biggest tech companies. Amazon became the third hyperscaler, after Alphabet and Meta, to report negative free cash flow, and S&P Global put Meta’s credit rating on negative outlook the week before, citing that same negative cash flow.

I believe a day of reckoning is coming for these companies. You can borrow your way through a buildout for a while, but debt has to be paid back, and at some point investors and lenders will want to see a return on all that spending. This week the market rewarded the companies with the biggest balance sheets, but soon the market will ask what was done with those massive balance sheets.

### Biggest loser this week

Tech was the mirror image of the winner. Communication services held the giants that can afford to wait out a slowdown. Tech held the companies that only get paid if the buildout keeps going: the makers of memory, storage and chips that fill data centers. Almost all of the week’s loss came Thursday, when the tech fund fell 8% in a single day and the semiconductor fund fell 12%. The worst damage was in memory and storage. Sandisk fell 22% and Micron 17% on Thursday; Western Digital and Seagate dropped about 16–17%. Nothing changed inside these companies. Sandisk had no news that day, and its data-center sales grew 61% last quarter. What changed was the outlook for their customers. Democrats who won in Texas, Georgia and Ohio ran on pausing or restricting data centers, and fewer data centers means fewer servers, and fewer servers means less memory. Reuters reported some data-center capacity due in 2028 could be at risk from construction pauses, and Citi flagged a rising number of pauses and cancellations. The election was the flip, but still isn’t the whole story. Sandisk was already down about 56% from its high before Thursday, and Micron about 37%. The question remains the same, will the gargantuan investment in AI produce returns. That’s the same question behind the biggest winner. This week the market punished the suppliers first. If the giants’ spending doesn’t start paying off, they’ll face the same question next.

## Wild Week in the Markets

The S&P 500 spent the week bouncing up and down. We saw a 1.58% jump after midterm results came in. The index was up around 1.4% up to that point. Thursday was the worst day since April 2025. The S&P was down 4.36%, the Nasdaq was down 5.5%, chip stocks down 12%, all 11 sectors in the red, and the VIX the volatility index jumped up 70%. The week ended with the S&P down 2.7%.

**Stocks.** The headline looks real simple the S&P dropped 2.7%, The Nasdaq 3.4%, and the Dow 0.9%. Underneath, the market split in two. Tech stocks fell 8.2% for the week and energy fell 12%. Meanwhile, communication services rose 4.9%, and real estate, consumer discretionary, financials, and consumer staples all finished higher. 5 of 11 sectors finished in the green in a week the index had its worse drop in over a year. Just goes to show how tech heavy theses indexes are weighted. Something to keep in mind for personal portfolios.

Two losers fell for opposite reasons. Tech fell on fear. Chip makers and neoclouds are the companies that need data centers to keep getting built, and Tuesday’s gubernatorial elections that saw states like Texas elect Democrats put that construction in doubt. Data center construction has been a contentious point on both sides of the aisle and Gina Hinojosa ran promising a moratorium on their construction. The semiconductor index fund SOXX fell 12% in one day off that and continued fear surrounding recent lackluster earnings reports. We saw money rotate from risky AI bets like chip makers and neoclouds to treasury bonds, defensive stocks, and cash rich AI. Energy fell on hope. Oil dropped 13% as a US-Iran deal started to look real. How many times have we heard that. Take it with a grain of salt. The winners rallied as they live on the other side of this story. Cheaper oil means more money in consumer’s pockets, which is why consumer discretionary rallied 2.4% on Monday alone. That split is why the index looked worse than the average stock. The S&P 500 is weighted by company size, and tech is its biggest chunk, so when tech drops the whole index drops with it.

**Government Bonds.** The 10-year Treasury yield fell from 4.94% to 4.86%, its fifth straight weekly drop. It’s now down about 42 basis points over five weeks. Quick refresher: A bond’s price and yield move inversely. When investors buy bonds, prices go up and yields go down. So, falling yields mean money is pouring into bonds. What is interesting is money flocked to bonds for two different reasons. First on Wednesday the 10yr dropped 8 basis points on account of oil dropping 8%. Cheaper oil means lower inflation, and lower inflation makes a bond’s fixed interest payments worth more, which incentivizes money to move into treasury bonds. On Thursday it fell again, 6 basis points to 4.83%, but this time out of fear. As the S&P had one of its worst days since the pandemic, money ran to the safest asset there is. Treasury bonds. Remember as demand increases prices go up and price and yield move inversely, hence the yield dropping 6 basis points. Wednesday people thought inflation is cooling. Thursday had people wanting to flee to safety. Friday’s strong job report pushed yields up as a hot economy is more likely to have hikes and that decreases the appeal of bonds at today’s rate. The election helped bonds a little too. A divided government can’t pass big spending bills, which means less borrowing and fewer new Treasuries flooding the market.

**Credit.** Corporate debt is where AI gets interesting. Building data centers takes enormous amounts of cash, and the Sevens Report says Thursday’s drop came down to fear that “companies reliant on credit and investor capital won’t get what they need.” The warning signs have been brewing all week. OpenAI cut its fundraising target by $10 billion, and a wave of capital raising announcements didn’t convince anyone the funding picture was getting better. Barclay’s credit puts it best; the AI race has changed. It used to that no company could slow down, they must keep shoveling money into the engine that is AI capex to keep up. Now, it’s a battle to see who is still standing. Simply put, the companies that can pay for data centers with their own cash can survive a slowdown while the ones that financed them with debt are in massive trouble. When lending to companies looks riskier, money runs to the safest borrower of all, the U.S. government, which is one more reason Treasury yields kept falling.

**Rates.** The Fed didn’t meet this week. Its key rate is still 3.75% to 4.00%. But the market spent the whole week arguing about its next move, and the 2yr Treasury yield is where you can watch that argument. The 10yr moves on inflation and fear. The 2yr moves almost entirely on what traders think the Fed will do in the next year or two. There were two forces that pulled in opposite directions. Thursday’s stock crash argued for fewer hikes, falling markets make borrowing harder and slow the economy on their own, doing some of the Fed’s work for it. Friday’s jobs report did the opposite. The economy added 165k jobs against 62k expected, unemployment fell to 4.1%, wages grew 3.1% from a year ago against 2.9% expected. A job market that strong pushes wages and therefore prices up, and inflation is already well above the Fed’s stated 2% target. Long story short the hot jobs report won out with the odds of a December hike rose to around 78%. But those two forces were battling it out in the minds of investors this past week. Barclays now expects hikes in both December and January. The election may make that easier: with a Democratic Senate able to block Fed appointments, there’s less political pressure on the Fed not to raise rates.

**Commodities.** Oil had its worst week in years. West Texas Intermediate, the U.S. benchmark, fell about 13% to $90.83 a barrel. Brent, the global benchmark, fell 13% to $98.32, its steepest weekly drop since the start of COVID. The reason is Iran. Oil had been priced for war: markets expected U.S. strikes to resume once the midterms were over (Sevens Report). Then the story flipped. On Monday the President said there was no post-election deadline to strike Iran, and both he and the Vice President touted progress in talks, so oil fell 8% that day. A small U.S. strike on Iran’s Qeshm Island Tuesday barely slowed it. On Wednesday, more headlines about diplomacy and little appetite in Washington to resume attacks knocked off another 8%. The election added pressure: Vice President Vance said “the reason we lost is the war.” When the risk of a supply disruption shrinks, traders stop paying extra for it. And on Monday this week came reports that Iran dropped its demand to control the Strait of Hormuz. Copper went the same way. Traders call it “Dr. Copper” because it goes into wiring, buildings and data centers, so its price diagnoses the economy. On Thursday gold rose 1% while copper fell 3.35% on fears of a global slowdown
